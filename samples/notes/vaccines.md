# How Vaccines Work

These notes explain the science behind vaccines: how the immune system recognises germs, how it remembers them, and how vaccines use that memory. They are general biology notes for learning, not medical advice. Questions about your own health or about a particular vaccine should always go to a qualified health professional.

## 1. Pathogens and the body's defences

A pathogen is a microorganism that can cause disease. The main types are bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites. Bacteria are single-celled organisms that can reproduce on their own. Viruses are much smaller and cannot reproduce by themselves: they must enter a living cell and use its machinery to make copies of themselves.

The body's first line of defence consists of physical and chemical barriers. The skin blocks most pathogens from getting in. Mucus in the nose and airways traps particles, and tiny hairs called cilia sweep it away. Stomach acid destroys many microorganisms that are swallowed, and tears contain enzymes that can break down bacteria.

If a pathogen gets past these barriers, the immune system responds.

## 2. Innate immunity: the fast, general response

The innate immune system reacts within minutes to hours. It does not target one specific pathogen; instead it recognises general warning signs that something foreign has entered the body.

White blood cells called phagocytes engulf and digest invaders. This process is called phagocytosis. Damaged tissues release chemical signals that cause inflammation: blood vessels widen, more blood flows to the area, and more immune cells arrive. This is why an infected cut can become red, warm and swollen. A raised body temperature, or fever, can also make conditions less favourable for some pathogens.

The innate response is quick, but it is the same every time. It does not improve with experience.

## 3. Adaptive immunity: the specific, learning response

The adaptive immune system is slower to start, usually taking several days, but it targets one particular pathogen very precisely, and it remembers.

Every pathogen carries molecules on its surface that the immune system can recognise. These molecules are called antigens. Each antigen has a particular shape.

Two main kinds of white blood cells, called lymphocytes, drive the adaptive response:

- **B cells** produce antibodies. An antibody is a Y-shaped protein whose tips fit one specific antigen, like a key fits a lock. Antibodies can stick to pathogens, which stops them entering cells and marks them for destruction by other immune cells.
- **T cells** have several roles. Helper T cells coordinate the response by activating B cells and other immune cells. Cytotoxic, or killer, T cells find and destroy the body's own cells that have been infected by a virus, which stops the virus from multiplying inside them.

When the body meets a new pathogen, only a few B cells and T cells happen to have receptors that match its antigens. Those cells are selected and then multiply rapidly to form a large army of identical cells. This is called clonal selection. Because this takes time, a person can feel ill during the first encounter with a pathogen before the adaptive response gets it under control.

## 4. Immune memory

After an infection has been cleared, most of the specialised cells die off. However, some remain as memory cells. Memory B cells and memory T cells can survive for years, and in some cases for a lifetime.

If the same pathogen enters the body again, the memory cells recognise it immediately. The second response, called the secondary response, is faster and stronger than the first, and it produces far more antibodies. Often the pathogen is destroyed before it can cause any symptoms. This protection is called immunity.

The difference between the first (primary) response and the secondary response is the key idea behind vaccination.

## 5. What a vaccine does

A vaccine trains the adaptive immune system to recognise a pathogen without the person having to catch the disease first. It does this by showing the immune system the pathogen's antigens in a safe form. The immune system responds by producing antibodies and, most importantly, memory cells. If the real pathogen arrives later, the body can mount a fast secondary response.

There are several ways to present antigens safely:

- **Inactivated vaccines** contain pathogens that have been killed, so they cannot cause disease.
- **Live attenuated vaccines** contain living pathogens that have been weakened so that they do not cause illness in healthy people. They often produce a strong, long-lasting response.
- **Subunit vaccines** contain only selected parts of the pathogen, such as particular proteins, rather than the whole organism.
- **Toxoid vaccines** are used when the illness is caused by a toxin that bacteria release. They contain an inactivated form of the toxin, so the body learns to neutralise it.
- **mRNA vaccines** contain genetic instructions that tell the body's own cells to make a harmless piece of the pathogen's protein for a short time. The immune system then responds to that protein. The mRNA itself breaks down naturally within days.

Some vaccines contain ingredients called adjuvants, which help to produce a stronger immune response to the antigen.

## 6. Doses and boosters

For some vaccines a single dose gives long-lasting protection. Others need more than one dose, given some weeks or months apart, to build up enough memory cells. Over time, protection from some vaccines gradually decreases, so a later dose, called a booster, is used to remind the immune system and strengthen the memory again.

Some pathogens change their antigens over time. Influenza viruses, for example, change gradually from year to year. Memory cells may then no longer recognise the new version well, which is why some vaccines are updated regularly.

## 7. Herd immunity

When a large proportion of a population is immune to a disease, it becomes difficult for the pathogen to spread from person to person, because it keeps reaching people who are immune. This indirectly protects people who are not immune, for example those who are too young to be vaccinated. This effect is called herd immunity, or community immunity.

The proportion of the population that needs to be immune depends on how contagious the disease is. The more easily a disease spreads, the higher the proportion that is needed to stop it spreading.

## 8. Testing and monitoring

Before a vaccine is approved for use, it goes through laboratory research and then clinical trials in several phases, each involving more volunteers than the last. The trials check whether the vaccine produces an immune response, whether it prevents disease and whether it is safe. Regulators review the evidence before approval, and vaccines continue to be monitored for rare side effects after they are in use.

## 9. Key points to remember

- Antigens are molecules on a pathogen that the immune system recognises; antibodies are proteins that bind to specific antigens.
- B cells make antibodies; helper T cells coordinate the response; killer T cells destroy infected cells.
- Memory cells make the secondary response faster and stronger than the primary response.
- Vaccines show the immune system antigens in a safe form so that memory cells form without the disease.
- Herd immunity protects people who cannot be immunised when enough of the population is immune.
