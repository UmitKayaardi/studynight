# Basics of Personal Finance

These notes explain basic personal finance concepts: budgeting, saving, interest, borrowing, inflation and risk. They describe how these ideas work in general and are not financial advice. For decisions about your own money, consider speaking to a qualified, independent adviser.

## 1. Income and spending

Income is money you receive, for example wages from a job. Gross income is the amount before deductions; net income, often called take-home pay, is what remains after tax and other deductions are taken off.

Spending can be divided into fixed costs and variable costs. **Fixed costs** stay roughly the same each month, such as rent or a phone contract. **Variable costs** change from month to month, such as food, transport and entertainment. It is also useful to separate **needs**, which are essential, from **wants**, which are nice to have but not essential.

## 2. Budgeting

A budget is a plan for how you will use your income over a period of time, usually a month. Making a budget starts with listing your net income and all your expected costs. If costs are higher than income, the difference must be covered by savings or borrowing, which is not sustainable for long.

One simple guideline is the 50/30/20 rule. It suggests dividing take-home pay so that about 50 per cent goes on needs, 30 per cent on wants, and 20 per cent on saving or paying off debt. It is a starting point rather than a strict rule, and the right proportions depend on each person's situation.

Tracking spending, even for a few weeks, often reveals small regular costs, such as unused subscriptions, that add up over time.

## 3. Saving and the emergency fund

Saving means setting aside part of your income instead of spending it. An important first goal is an **emergency fund**: money kept in an easily accessible account to cover unexpected costs, such as a broken laptop or a sudden drop in income. A common suggestion is to build up enough to cover three to six months of essential expenses.

Paying yourself first is a habit where a fixed amount is moved into savings as soon as income arrives, rather than saving whatever is left at the end of the month.

## 4. Interest

Interest is the price of money over time. When you save, a bank pays you interest. When you borrow, you pay interest to the lender. Interest is usually given as an annual percentage rate.

With **simple interest**, interest is calculated only on the original amount, called the principal. With **compound interest**, interest is also earned on interest that has already been added. Over long periods, compounding makes a large difference.

The value of savings with annual compounding can be calculated with the formula:

$$A = P(1 + r)^t$$

Here $P$ is the principal, $r$ is the annual interest rate written as a decimal, $t$ is the number of years, and $A$ is the final amount. For example, £1,000 saved at 5 per cent a year for 2 years grows to £1,000 × 1.05 × 1.05 = £1,102.50. With simple interest it would grow to only £1,100.

A useful shortcut is the **rule of 72**: dividing 72 by the annual interest rate in per cent gives a rough estimate of how many years it takes for money to double. At 6 per cent, money doubles in about 12 years.

## 5. Inflation

Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. When there is inflation, the same amount of money buys fewer goods and services than before; in other words, its **purchasing power** falls.

The real return on savings is roughly the interest rate minus the inflation rate. If savings earn 3 per cent while inflation is 4 per cent, the real return is about minus 1 per cent, and the money can buy slightly less each year even though the number in the account goes up.

## 6. Borrowing and credit

Borrowing lets people buy something now and pay for it later, but it usually costs money in interest and fees. The **annual percentage rate (APR)** includes both the interest and the compulsory fees, which makes it easier to compare the total cost of different loans.

A **credit card** lets you borrow up to a limit. If the full balance is paid off each month, many cards charge no interest on purchases. If only part of the balance is paid, interest is charged on the rest, often at a high rate. Paying only the minimum amount each month can make a debt last for many years and cost much more than the original purchase.

A **credit score** is a number that lenders use to estimate how likely a person is to repay borrowed money. It is influenced by things such as paying bills on time and how much of the available credit is being used.

## 7. Risk, return and diversification

Different places to keep money offer different combinations of risk and possible return. Cash in a savings account is low risk but usually offers a low return. Investments such as shares can offer higher returns over long periods, but their value can go down as well as up.

In general, investments with a higher potential return come with a higher risk. **Diversification** means spreading money across many different investments, so that a loss in one has a smaller effect on the total. The idea is often summed up as "don't put all your eggs in one basket".

The **time horizon** is how long money can stay invested. Money that will be needed soon is usually kept in lower-risk places, because there may not be time to recover from a fall in value.

## 8. Avoiding common traps

- Offers that promise high returns with no risk are a warning sign of a scam.
- Impulse purchases can be reduced by waiting a day or two before buying non-essential items.
- Comparing the total cost of a loan, not just the monthly payment, avoids underestimating what borrowing costs.

## 9. Key points to remember

- Net income is income after tax and other deductions.
- A budget compares income with planned spending; the 50/30/20 rule is a simple starting guideline.
- An emergency fund covers unexpected costs; three to six months of essential expenses is a common target.
- Compound interest earns interest on interest; $A = P(1 + r)^t$.
- Inflation reduces purchasing power; the real return is roughly the interest rate minus inflation.
- Higher potential returns usually come with higher risk; diversification spreads risk.
