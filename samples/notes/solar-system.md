# The Solar System

These notes give an overview of the Solar System: how it formed, the Sun at its centre, the eight planets, and the smaller bodies that share our neighbourhood. They are written as revision notes for an introductory astronomy course.

## 1. What the Solar System is

The Solar System is the Sun together with everything that is held in orbit around it by gravity. That includes eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets such as Pluto, millions of asteroids, comets and a great deal of dust and gas. The Sun is by far the biggest member: it contains about 99.8 per cent of all the mass in the Solar System. Everything else, including giant Jupiter, makes up the remaining tiny fraction.

Distances in the Solar System are so large that kilometres become awkward. Astronomers therefore use the astronomical unit (AU), which is the average distance between the Earth and the Sun, roughly 150 million kilometres. Mercury orbits at about 0.4 AU, while Neptune, the most distant planet, orbits at about 30 AU.

## 2. How it formed

The leading explanation for the origin of the Solar System is the nebular hypothesis. About 4.6 billion years ago, a cloud of gas and dust, called a nebula, began to collapse under its own gravity. A nearby disturbance, possibly the shock wave from an exploding star, may have triggered the collapse.

As the cloud shrank it began to spin faster, in the same way a figure skater spins faster when pulling in their arms. The spinning flattened the cloud into a disc. Most of the material fell to the centre, where it became hot and dense enough for nuclear fusion to begin: the Sun was born.

In the disc around the young Sun, small grains of dust stuck together to form larger and larger clumps. These building blocks are called planetesimals. Over millions of years, planetesimals collided and merged to form the planets. Close to the Sun it was too hot for ice to survive, so the inner planets formed mainly from rock and metal. Further out, beyond a boundary called the frost line, water and other substances could freeze into ice. More solid material was available there, which allowed the outer planets to grow large enough to capture huge amounts of hydrogen and helium gas.

This history explains why all the planets orbit the Sun in the same direction and in nearly the same flat plane.

## 3. The Sun

The Sun is a star, a giant ball of hot gas made mostly of hydrogen and helium. In its core, the temperature reaches about 15 million degrees Celsius. At these conditions hydrogen nuclei fuse together to form helium, releasing enormous amounts of energy. This process is called nuclear fusion, and it is the source of the sunlight that warms the Earth.

Energy produced in the core takes a very long time to reach the surface, because it is absorbed and re-emitted many times on the way out. The visible surface of the Sun is called the photosphere and has a temperature of about 5,500 degrees Celsius. Darker, cooler patches on the photosphere are called sunspots. The number of sunspots rises and falls in a cycle that lasts about 11 years.

The Sun also releases a constant stream of charged particles into space, known as the solar wind. When these particles reach the Earth, most are deflected by the planet's magnetic field, but some are channelled towards the poles, where they make the air glow and create the aurora.

## 4. The inner, rocky planets

The four planets closest to the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars. They are called terrestrial planets because they have solid, rocky surfaces like the Earth.

**Mercury** is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. It has almost no atmosphere to trap heat, so its daytime side becomes extremely hot while its night side becomes extremely cold. Its surface is covered in craters, a little like our Moon.

**Venus** is similar in size to the Earth, but conditions there are very different. Its thick atmosphere is made mostly of carbon dioxide, which traps heat through a strong greenhouse effect. As a result, Venus is the hottest planet, even though Mercury is closer to the Sun. Venus also spins in the opposite direction to most planets, and very slowly: a day on Venus is longer than its year.

**Earth** is the only planet known to support life. Liquid water covers about 70 per cent of its surface. Its atmosphere, rich in nitrogen and oxygen, protects life from harmful radiation and keeps temperatures fairly stable. Earth has one large natural satellite, the Moon.

**Mars** is known as the red planet because iron oxide, or rust, in its soil gives it a reddish colour. It has a thin atmosphere of carbon dioxide. Mars has the largest volcano in the Solar System, Olympus Mons, and a huge canyon system called Valles Marineris. Dried-up river channels suggest that liquid water once flowed on its surface. Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos.

## 5. The outer giants

Beyond Mars lie four much larger planets. They are divided into two groups.

**Jupiter** and **Saturn** are gas giants, made mostly of hydrogen and helium. Jupiter is the largest planet; more than a thousand Earths could fit inside it. Its Great Red Spot is a storm larger than the Earth that has been observed for centuries. Saturn is famous for its bright rings, which are made of countless pieces of ice and rock, ranging from tiny grains to chunks as big as houses. Saturn is less dense than water.

**Uranus** and **Neptune** are often called ice giants. Besides hydrogen and helium, they contain large amounts of water, ammonia and methane in icy forms. Methane in their atmospheres absorbs red light, which gives both planets a blue colour. Uranus is unusual because it is tipped on its side, so it appears to roll around the Sun. Neptune has the fastest winds measured on any planet.

All four giant planets have ring systems, although only Saturn's are easy to see, and all four have many moons. Some of these moons are remarkable worlds in their own right. Jupiter's moon Europa is thought to hide an ocean of liquid water beneath its icy crust, and Saturn's largest moon, Titan, has a thick atmosphere and lakes of liquid methane.

## 6. Smaller bodies

**Dwarf planets** orbit the Sun and are round, but unlike planets they have not cleared their orbital path of other objects. Pluto was classified as a planet until 2006, when it was reclassified as a dwarf planet. Ceres, the largest object in the asteroid belt, is also a dwarf planet.

**Asteroids** are rocky or metallic objects, mostly found in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. Jupiter's strong gravity is thought to have prevented the material in this region from ever forming a planet.

**Comets** are made of ice, dust and rock. They spend most of their time far from the Sun. When a comet's orbit brings it closer to the Sun, the ice begins to turn into gas, forming a glowing cloud called the coma and often a long tail. A comet's tail always points away from the Sun, because it is pushed by the solar wind and by sunlight.

Far beyond Neptune lies the **Kuiper Belt**, a region of icy bodies that includes Pluto. Even further out, the **Oort Cloud** is thought to be a vast, spherical shell of icy objects surrounding the whole Solar System. It is believed to be the source of comets that take thousands of years to complete one orbit.

## 7. Orbits and gravity

Planets move around the Sun in paths called orbits, which are slightly stretched circles known as ellipses. The time a planet takes to complete one orbit is its orbital period, or year. Planets closer to the Sun move faster and have shorter years. Mercury completes an orbit in about 88 Earth days, while Neptune takes about 165 Earth years.

The force that keeps the planets in their orbits is gravity. The strength of the gravitational pull between two objects depends on their masses and on the distance between them: more massive objects pull more strongly, and the pull becomes weaker as the distance increases. Without the Sun's gravity, each planet would travel off into space in a straight line.

## 8. Key points to remember

- The Sun contains about 99.8 per cent of the Solar System's mass.
- The Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a collapsing cloud of gas and dust.
- The inner planets are small and rocky; the outer planets are large and made mostly of gas and ice.
- Venus is the hottest planet because of its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere.
- Pluto has been classified as a dwarf planet since 2006.
- Comet tails point away from the Sun.
