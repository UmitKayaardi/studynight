# The Printing Press and the Spread of Ideas

## Before printing

Before the printing press reached Europe, books were copied by hand, often by scribes working in monasteries. Copying a single book could take months, so books were rare and expensive, and most people never owned one. Errors crept in each time a text was copied.

Printing with carved wooden blocks had been used in East Asia for centuries, and movable type made of ceramic and later metal had been developed in China and Korea well before it appeared in Europe.

## Gutenberg's press

Around 1450, Johannes Gutenberg, working in the German city of Mainz, developed a printing system that combined several inventions. He used movable metal type: individual letters cast in metal that could be arranged into lines and pages and then reused. He adapted the screw press, a machine already used for pressing grapes and olives, to press paper evenly onto the inked type. He also developed an oil-based ink that stuck well to metal type.

Gutenberg's most famous work is a printed Bible, completed in the mid-1450s. Around 180 copies were made.

## Rapid spread

Printing spread quickly. Within fifty years of Gutenberg's first books, print shops had opened in many European cities. Books printed before 1501 are called incunabula. Because a print shop could produce hundreds of identical copies, the price of books fell sharply.

## Effects on society

Cheaper books helped more people learn to read. Identical copies meant that scholars in different places could refer to exactly the same text, which made it easier to compare ideas and correct mistakes. Printing also helped to standardise spelling and grammar in many languages.

Printed pamphlets allowed new ideas to spread far faster than before. During the Protestant Reformation in the sixteenth century, Martin Luther's writings were printed and distributed widely in a short time. Printing also supported the growth of science, because researchers could share detailed descriptions, diagrams and data with a wide audience.
