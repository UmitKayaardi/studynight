# Plate Tectonics

## The structure of the Earth

The Earth is made of layers. The outermost layer is the crust, which is thin compared with the rest of the planet. Below it is the mantle, a thick layer of hot rock that can flow very slowly over long periods of time. At the centre is the core, which has a liquid outer part and a solid inner part, both made mainly of iron and nickel.

The crust and the uppermost part of the mantle together form the lithosphere. The lithosphere is rigid and is broken into large pieces called tectonic plates. The plates rest on a softer layer of the mantle called the asthenosphere.

## Why plates move

Heat from the Earth's interior drives slow currents in the mantle, called convection currents. Hot rock rises, spreads sideways and cools, then sinks again. These movements, together with the pull of sinking plate edges, cause the plates to move. Plates move only a few centimetres per year, roughly as fast as fingernails grow.

## Plate boundaries

There are three main kinds of plate boundary.

At a **divergent boundary**, plates move apart. Magma rises to fill the gap and forms new crust. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is an example: the Atlantic Ocean is slowly getting wider.

At a **convergent boundary**, plates move towards each other. When an ocean plate meets a continental plate, the denser ocean plate sinks beneath the other in a process called subduction. Subduction zones produce deep ocean trenches and chains of volcanoes. When two continental plates collide, neither sinks easily, so the crust is pushed upwards to form high mountain ranges such as the Himalayas.

At a **transform boundary**, plates slide past each other sideways. Crust is neither created nor destroyed, but friction makes the plates stick and then suddenly slip, causing earthquakes. The San Andreas Fault in California is a transform boundary.

## Evidence for moving continents

In the early twentieth century, Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined in a single supercontinent, which he called Pangaea. His evidence included the way the coastlines of South America and Africa fit together, matching fossils on continents now separated by oceans, and matching rock layers. Many scientists rejected the idea at first because Wegener could not explain what force moved the continents. The discovery of sea-floor spreading in the 1960s provided the missing mechanism.
